Sacred Temples

Sankat Mochan Temple

Southern Varanasi, near Durgakund and the Assi rivulet

The welcoming gate of Sankat Mochan Temple, where the poet Tulsidas is held to have received his vision of Hanuman.

Nestled amidst verdant banyan and neem groves on the southern outskirts of Varanasi, the Sankat Mochan Temple is dedicated to Lord Hanuman in his aspect as the "Reliever of Troubles" (Sankat Mochan). Established in the early sixteenth century by the poet-saint Goswami Tulsidas, the temple represents one of North India’s most cherished centers of everyday popular devotion, renowned equally for its inclusive spirituality and its legendary music festival.

The Vision of Goswami Tulsidas

Tradition records that during the sixteenth century, Goswami Tulsidas lived as a renunicate in Varanasi while writing the Ramcharitmanas. Local folklore tells that after bathing daily at the Assi river, the poet poured the leftover water at the base of a withered acacia tree. An ascetic spirit inhabiting the tree was touched by the offering and offered Tulsidas a boon. The poet requested only one thing: to behold Lord Rama face to face.

The spirit instructed Tulsidas that Hanuman attended the poet's daily recitations disguised as an elderly, afflicted leper sitting humbly in the back row. Tulsidas identified the disguised visitor, followed him into the dense woodland grove that occupied this site, and clung to his feet until the god revealed his celestial form. On the exact spot where Hanuman appeared, Tulsidas consecrated an earthen idol, establishing the sanctuary that has comforted millions through successive centuries.

Key Historical Facts

  • Presiding Deity: Lord Hanuman, oriented uniquely facing Lord Rama
  • Founder: Poet-saint Goswami Tulsidas (c. 1500–1623 CE)
  • Rebuilt Structure: Expanded in the early twentieth century by educationist Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya
  • Cultural Institution: Host of the historic Sankat Mochan Sangeet Samaroh, held every April since 1923
  • Sacred Offering: Besan laddus prepared daily with pure ghee inside temple kitchens

Spiritual Practice & The Iconic Besan Laddu

The consecrated idol of Hanuman within the inner sanctum is unique in iconographic design: rather than facing outward directly towards the congregants, the murti stands turned slightly toward the right, looking continuously across to an adjacent shrine enshrining his master, Lord Rama. Devotees regard Hanuman here not merely as an awe-inspiring celestial protector, but as an affectionate elder brother and intermediary who listens attentively to everyday sorrows.

Tuesdays and Saturdays bring tens of thousands of worshippers bearing garlands of vermilion-dipped betel leaves (paan) and marigolds. A signature element of the temple visit is its celebrated besan ke laddu—fragrant, golden chickpea-flour sweets prepared fresh in the temple’s vast artisanal kitchens using traditional pure desi ghee. Devotees leave offerings before the deity and distribute the consecrated sweets among family and visitors as auspicious blessings.

Sankat Mochan Sangeet Samaroh: A Centennial Cultural Legacy

The temple is celebrated worldwide for hosting the annual Sankat Mochan Sangeet Samaroh, a prestigious six-night classical music and dance festival organized during the Hindu festival of Hanuman Jayanti (typically April). Initiated in 1923 by the temple's hereditary Mahant, Pandit Amrit Lal Mishra, the festival requires that all artists perform without remuneration as an offering of devotion (haaziri) before the deity.

For a century, every titan of Indian classical arts—including Ustad Bismillah Khan, Pandit Ravi Shankar, Ustad Zakir Hussain, Girija Devi, Pandit Jasraj, and Pandit Hariprasad Chaurasia—has performed in the open courtyard before audiences that sit together on carpets until dawn. Ustad Bismillah Khan, a devout Muslim shehnai maestro, famously performed his morning prayers and offered ragas here regularly, exemplifying Varanasi’s deeply intertwined syncretic Ganga-Jamuni tehzeeb.

Visitor Information & Guidelines

Timings
Open daily from 05:00 to 22:00. Temple doors close briefly between 12:00 and 15:00 for the afternoon bhog ritual.
Security & Regulations
Mobile phones, electronic gadgets, leather belts, and cameras are strictly forbidden inside the temple perimeter. Reliable shoe and locker stalls are stationed near the main gate.
Monkeys Warning
A large troop of rhesus macaque monkeys inhabits the temple grove. Keep spectacles, food items, and prasad bags tucked inside opaque bags or beneath shawls.
Getting There
Located on Sankat Mochan Road, easily accessible by auto-rickshaw or taxi, approximately 3 km south of the central railway station.

Cultural Note: The best time to experience the temple’s vibrant communal spirit without extreme festival crowds is early on a weekday morning around 06:30 during the morning aarti.
